Start with the basics

What does a medical coder actually do?

Medical coders review healthcare documentation and translate documented diagnoses, procedures, services, and supplies into standardized medical codes. Those codes support accurate reporting, reimbursement, data analysis, and healthcare operations.

Coding is not simply looking up a diagnosis in a book. A coder must understand the medical record, apply official coding conventions and guidelines, select codes at the appropriate level of specificity, and know when documentation supports—or does not support—a particular code.

Beginner takeaway: Good medical coding combines clinical understanding, code-book navigation, guideline knowledge, attention to detail, and critical thinking.

Medical coding vs. medical billing: what’s the difference?

Medical coding and medical billing are closely connected, but they are not the same job. Coding focuses primarily on interpreting documentation and assigning appropriate diagnosis and procedure codes. Billing focuses more heavily on the financial claim cycle—such as insurance verification, claim submission, payment posting, denials, follow-up, and accounts receivable.

Some jobs combine aspects of both, especially in smaller practices, but someone deciding on a career path should understand that learning coding and learning billing involve different skill sets.

Do you need a degree to become a medical coder?

A four-year college degree is not the only route into medical coding. What matters is developing the knowledge and practical coding skills employers need. For a beginner, that usually means learning medical terminology, anatomy, pathophysiology, coding guidelines, and how to use the major medical code sets correctly.

Professional certification can also be an important part of demonstrating coding knowledge. The right path depends on your background, career goals, learning style, and the type of coding work you hope to pursue.

Build the foundation

What do you need to learn to become a medical coder?

Clinical foundation

Medical terminology, anatomy, and pathophysiology help you understand what is documented in a patient’s record.

Code sets

Professional coding students commonly learn ICD-10-CM for diagnoses, CPT® for physician and outpatient procedures/services, and HCPCS Level II for supplies, products, and certain services.

Guidelines & application

You need more than memorization. Coders learn conventions, guidelines, modifiers, documentation requirements, and how to apply them to real scenarios.

One of the most important beginner skills is learning how to navigate the code books. Medical coders do not need to memorize every code. They need to know how to find, verify, interpret, and apply the correct information efficiently.

What is CPC certification?

The Certified Professional Coder (CPC®) credential is a professional medical coding certification focused on coding services in the professional/physician setting. CPC preparation includes ICD-10-CM diagnosis coding, CPT® procedure coding, HCPCS Level II, modifiers, coding guidelines, medical terminology, anatomy, and related regulatory concepts.

The current CPC exam contains 100 multiple-choice questions and allows four hours. AAPC states that candidates must answer at least 70% correctly to pass. The exam tests application of ICD-10-CM, CPT®, and HCPCS Level II, and candidates may reference approved code books during the exam.

Certification requirements and exam policies can change. Always verify current exam rules directly with the credentialing organization before testing.

Your roadmap

How to become a medical coder step by step

Learn what the career actually involves

Before paying for a program, understand the difference between coding and billing, the types of coding roles available, and what a coder does during a normal workday.

Build your medical foundation

Study medical terminology, anatomy, and pathophysiology. These subjects help you understand clinical documentation instead of treating coding as simple code lookup.

Learn the major code sets and guidelines

Develop practical skills using ICD-10-CM, CPT®, and HCPCS Level II, including conventions, guidelines, modifiers, and code-book navigation.

Practice applying what you learn

Work through coding questions and realistic documentation. Practice should include explaining why an answer is correct—not merely checking an answer key.

Prepare for professional certification

If CPC certification fits your career goals, move into focused exam preparation once you have built the underlying coding knowledge. A study guide alone should not be treated as a substitute for learning coding from the beginning.

Begin building experience

Certification is an important milestone, but career development continues afterward. Look for opportunities to strengthen coding accuracy, documentation interpretation, specialty knowledge, and real-world application.

How should a beginner choose medical coding training?

There is no single learning format that works for everyone. Some students prefer self-paced study because they need flexibility. Others learn better with live instruction, deadlines, instructor feedback, and the ability to ask questions in real time.

Before choosing a program, look beyond the price and ask what you will actually receive. Does the training teach concepts from the beginning? Does it include guided coding practice? Can you ask an instructor questions? Does it explain why codes are selected? Does the schedule fit your life?

Watch for this common mistake: CPC exam review materials are valuable once you have learned coding, but review materials are not the same thing as beginner coding education.

Will AI replace medical coders?

Technology and artificial intelligence are changing coding workflows, but that makes strong coding knowledge—not less knowledge—important. Coding involves interpreting documentation, applying guidelines, recognizing unsupported assumptions, resolving ambiguity, and following payer, organizational, and client-specific requirements.

Automated tools can assist with parts of the process, but trained professionals are still needed to validate coding decisions, understand context, identify documentation problems, and help ensure coding is accurate and compliant. Beginners should learn to work effectively with technology while developing the judgment needed to review its output.

Frequently asked questions

Medical coding questions beginners often ask

Do I need healthcare experience before learning medical coding?

Prior healthcare experience can be helpful, but beginners can build the necessary foundation through structured education in medical terminology, anatomy, pathophysiology, and coding.

Do I have to memorize all the medical codes?

No. Effective coders learn how to navigate coding resources, follow guidelines, verify codes, and apply them accurately. Familiarity grows with practice.

Should I learn medical billing and coding together?

They are related but distinct skill sets. Understanding both can be useful, but you should choose training based on the role you want and avoid assuming billing and coding are interchangeable.

Can I learn medical coding online?

Yes. Online learning can be self-paced or instructor-led. The best choice depends on whether you thrive with flexibility or benefit from structure, live explanation, feedback, and accountability.
